**1. What is the scientific name of your chosen plant and its plastid genome accession number?**

The scientific name is Dracaena draco L. (dragon tree). The plastid/chloroplast genome accession number shown in the reference study is MN990038. 

**2. What is the total length of the plastid genome in base pairs?**

The total length of the plastid genome is 155,422 bp. 

**3. Can you identify the LSC, SSC, IRa, and IRb regions on your map? Briefly describe their positions.**

- LSC (Large Single Copy) – the large region at the top of the map.

- SSC (Small Single Copy) – the smaller region at the bottom.

- IRa (Inverted Repeat a) – the repeated region on the right side.

- IRb (Inverted Repeat b) – the repeated region on the left side.

The LSC is about 83,942 bp, the SSC is about 18,472 bp, and each IR is about 26,504 bp.

**4. Give three examples of genes located in the LSC region.**

- rbcL – codes for the large subunit of Rubisco.

- atpB – codes for a subunit of ATP synthase.

- psbA – codes for a Photosystem II reaction-center protein.
 
**5. Give at least one example of a gene located in the SSC region.** 

One example is ndhF, which codes for a subunit of the NADH dehydrogenase complex. The SSC region contains several ndh genes involved in photosynthetic electron transport. 

**6. Give at least one example of a gene that occurs within an inverted repeat region. Is the gene shown more than once because of the duplicated IR regions?**

An example is rpl2, which occurs in the inverted repeat region. Yes, genes located within the duplicated IR regions can appear twice, once in each IR. The D. draco genome contains duplicated genes such as rpl2, rps7, rps12, rps19, and several rRNA and tRNA genes. 

**7. Choose one photosynthesis-related gene visible on your map. State its gene name and its general biological function.** 

One example is psbA. It codes for a core protein of Photosystem II, which is involved in the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis and helps in the process of converting light energy into chemical energy. 

**8. What does the direction of the gene arrows or gene orientation tell you about transcription on the plastid genome?**

The direction of the arrows shows the direction in which the genes are transcribed. In the map, genes pointing inward are transcribed clockwise, while genes pointing outward are transcribed counterclockwise. Therefore, the plastid genome contains genes transcribed in both directions. 

**9. Look at the GC content graph. Does GC content appear uniform across the entire plastid genome, or does it vary among regions? Describe what you observe without over-interpreting it.** 

The GC content is not completely uniform across the plastid genome. The graph shows some variation between regions. In the published D. draco genome, GC content was approximately 35.6% in the LSC, 31.2% in the SSC, and 42.9% in the IR regions, with an overall GC content of 37.6%. Thus, the IR regions appear relatively higher in GC content, while the SSC is lower. 

**10. Why is a graphical plastid genome map useful compared with viewing only the raw DNA sequence or a long list of annotations?** 

A graphical plastid genome map is useful because it allows us to quickly see the locations, orientations, and types of genes, as well as the LSC, SSC, and IR regions. It also makes it easier to observe the overall genome organization and GC-content pattern than when looking only at a long DNA sequence or annotation list.
